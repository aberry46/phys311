Honestly the hardest concept that we have had to deal with thus far has been translating the physics of a collision into code that ACTUALLY behaves correctly within every single situation. The equations themselves have not been necessarily difficult, but moving them into a simple 1D collision with multiple objects and then into two dimensions required quite a bit of thinking and planning. Specifically, having to separate the collision detection from the collision response was absolutely a nightmare. Then, having to check whether the objects were actually approaching one another and having to decompose their velocities into normal and/or tangential components took quite a long time.

In a positive light however, I did enjoy seeing the importance of defining a system prior to applying a known conservation law. Particularly with the Box Simulation, I believe there to be constant momentum of the particles, but initially failed to consider how the walls might affect the particles via external impulses. Once I included the momentum that was transferred to the walls, I had found a complete system with conserved momentum (minus unavoidable numerical precision). Overall, I enjoyed seeing conservation laws not simply acting as equations but as tools for understanding why a model may or may not make physical sense.

One other thing I wanted to mention was that I enjoyed seeing the gas in a box simulation develop from the code that I had already produced. Using every particle at the same speed and watching them diverge into a distribution of speeds actually helped me understand why temperature is an average of kinetic energies within a system of particles. At this point, I find myself not only learning new topics, but I am learning why certain phenomena is the way it is, and how to model it via code (which is pretty cool if you ask me)!
